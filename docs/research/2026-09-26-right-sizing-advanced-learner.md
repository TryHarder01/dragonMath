# Research: right-sizing for an advanced young learner

- **Date:** 2026-09-26
- **Status:** Decided. Implemented in Egg Warmer (facts within 20) and Egg Crates (multiplication).
- **Source note:** Written from the established literature (see Sources), not from a new literature search.
- **Builds on:** [2026-09-26-early-math-pedagogy.md](2026-09-26-early-math-pedagogy.md)
- **Revisit when:** he's fluent with all ×1–×10 facts, or the level data in the parent corner shows him stuck or bored.

## The child

- Adds within 20 (up to 10 + 10). **Subtraction is weak** (parent report).
- Knows roughly a quarter to a half of the 10×10 multiplication table. That's probably ×1, ×2, ×5 and ×10 plus some others. We don't know exactly.
- This is late 1st / 2nd grade work, reaching into 3rd grade. The kindergarten ladders in the original design (counting 1–10, subitizing) are far too easy.

## Decision

1. **Same learning-trajectory approach, further along the trajectories.** Target:
   - addition and subtraction strategies within 20 (1.OA.6)
   - the relationship between + and − (1.OA.4, 1.OA.8)
   - multiplication as equal groups and arrays (2.OA.4, 3.OA.1)
   - fluency with ×1–×10 facts (3.OA.7)
2. **Fast placement.** On a fresh profile, each game moves up a level after **every** first-try correct answer, until the first miss. After that the normal rule applies (up after 3 in a row, down after 2 misses). He reaches his real level within a few problems instead of grinding through easy levels.
3. **Longer ladders (8 levels).** The bottom levels still work for a younger sibling.

## What the research says at this stage

### Fluency comes from strategies, not rote drill
- Children who reason with **derived-fact strategies** reach fact fluency more reliably and remember facts longer than children drilled on isolated facts (Baroody, 2006). Examples:
  - make ten: 8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3
  - doubles and near-doubles: 6 + 7 = 6 + 6 + 1
  - think-addition for subtraction: 13 − 5 → 5 + ? = 13
- The recommended order for addition facts is +0/+1, doubles, make-ten and bridging through 10, then near-doubles, then the rest. Subtraction is taught as the inverse of addition (Van de Walle et al.).
- **Our hint model:** a double ten-frame. The second addend fills the first frame to 10 and spills into the second, so the child *sees* make-ten. Subtraction crosses dots out while counting back. Missing-part problems fill in the gap.

### Multiplication: groups, then arrays, then derived facts
- Young children understand multiplication first as **equal groups** ("3 nests with 4 eggs each"), then as **arrays** (rows × columns). Arrays make the turnaround property (3×4 = 4×3) and splitting visible, which later becomes the area model (Barmby et al., 2009; Young-Loveridge, 2005).
- **Order of facts** (common in research-based curricula):
  1. ×2, ×10, ×5 (skip counting)
  2. ×1 and ×0
  3. ×3 and ×4 (doubling: ×4 = double ×2)
  4. ×9 (a pattern)
  5. ×6, ×7, ×8, derived from known facts: 7×6 = 5×6 + 2×6
- Turnarounds halve the facts left to learn once he knows one direction.
- **Our hint model:** an egg array with row-by-row skip counting ("4, 8, 12…"). For ×6–×9 it splits off the first 5 rows ("5 rows of 7 is 35, and 2 more rows…").
- **Division enters as a missing factor:** "How many rows of 4 make 20?" (3.OA.4, 3.OA.6).

### No timed tests, even for a fast kid
- Timed fact tests are associated with math anxiety, including in high achievers, and push children toward memorising rather than reasoning (Boaler, 2014; Ramirez et al., 2013).
- We keep the no-timer rule. Fluency shows up as accuracy with less and less need for the picture, not as speed.

### Mixing and spacing
- **Interleaved practice** (mixing problem types) beats blocked practice for long-term retention and for choosing the right strategy (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007; Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic, 2015).
- The top level of each game mixes all its problem types. Egg Warmer's top level also mixes in easy multiplication.

### Advanced learners
- For advanced young learners the evidence favours content acceleration (working at their actual level) over repeating material they already know (Assouline et al., *A Nation Empowered*, 2015).
- Aim for the same ~80% success rate. For him that means harder content, not the same content faster.

## Level ladders

**Egg Warmer: subtraction within 20, built on addition he already knows**

Parent update (same day): addition is solid, but **subtraction is weak**. So most of the ladder is small subtraction steps, and addition is a warm-up. The ladder follows the usual subtraction progression: take away small amounts, then subtract from teens without crossing ten, then "think addition" (subtraction as a missing part, 1.OA.4), then crossing ten.
1. Addition warm-up within 20, number sentence only (placement moves him past this quickly)
2. Take away within 10, with a picture (dots crossed out while counting back)
3. Teens without crossing ten (17 − 4, 15 − 5), with a picture
4. Think addition: 8 + ? = 13, with a picture (the bridge from his strength to his weak spot)
5. Subtract crossing ten (13 − 5), with a picture. The hint takes the ones away, then takes the rest from the ten.
6. Subtract within 20, number sentence only (picture in the hint)
7. Fact families: mixed + / − / missing part within 20, number sentence only
8. Mixed + / − within 20, plus ×2, ×5 and ×10 facts (interleaving)

**Egg Crates: multiplication**
1. Equal groups: 3 nests of 4 eggs
2. Arrays ×2 and ×10, with a picture
3. ×5 (mixed with ×2 and ×10), with a picture
4. ×3 and ×4, with a picture
5. ×1–×5 and ×10, number sentence only
6. ×6–×9, with a picture and a split-at-5 hint
7. Missing factor: ? × 4 = 20
8. Mixed, all facts to 10 × 10
